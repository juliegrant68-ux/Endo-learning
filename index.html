Age-Related Oesophageal Dysmotility

Presbyoesophagus describes the age-related decline in oesophageal motility, becoming increasingly prevalent over the age of 60. Changes in smooth muscle, neural control, and connective tissue collectively impair the coordinated peristalsis required for safe swallowing and bolus transit.

⚙ Pathophysiology

  • Reduced peristaltic amplitude — weakened primary and secondary contractions
  • Impaired relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS)
  • Loss of inhibitory neurones in the myenteric plexus with age
  • Increased non-peristaltic contractions — simultaneous, repetitive, or retrograde
  • Reduced oesophageal clearance — prolonged bolus transit time
  • Connective tissue changes reducing wall compliance

◉ Signs & Symptoms

  • Dysphagia — particularly to solids; may be intermittent
  • Odynophagia — painful swallowing, especially with large boluses
  • Chest pain — non-cardiac; may mimic angina
  • Globus sensation — persistent feeling of a lump in the throat
  • Regurgitation — of undigested food or liquid
  • Weight loss — secondary to reduced intake and fear of eating
  • Aspiration risk — particularly in those with concurrent neurological conditions

🔍 Corkscrew Oesophagus (Diffuse Oesophageal Spasm)

The corkscrew appearance can be seen on two investigations:

Barium swallow fluoroscopy — simultaneous, non-peristaltic contractions produce a segmented, spiral or "rosary bead" contour as contrast passes through the oesophagus. These tertiary contractions are uncoordinated and non-propulsive, occurring across multiple segments simultaneously.

OGD (oesophagogastroduodenoscopy) — the characteristic corkscrew or spiral mucosal appearance may also be visible endoscopically, with the oesophageal wall showing rings or corrugations produced by simultaneous circular muscle contractions during insertion of the scope.

The pattern is associated with diffuse oesophageal spasm (DOS) and in the elderly may represent an extreme manifestation of presbyoesophagus. High-resolution manometry (HRM) remains the gold standard for functional characterisation.

✦ Diagnostic Pathway

Step 1 Clinical History Symptom onset, duration, dietary modification, weight loss, aspiration events
Step 2 Endoscopy (OGD) Exclude structural causes; corkscrew/spiral mucosal appearance may be visible during insertion
Step 3 Barium Swallow Dynamic assessment of bolus transit; may reveal corkscrew pattern or tertiary contractions
Step 4 High-Resolution Manometry Gold standard for motility classification using Chicago Classification v4.0
Step 5 pH / Impedance If reflux component suspected; 24-hour ambulatory monitoring

≡ Severity Grading

● Mild
  • ▸Intermittent dysphagia to solids only
  • ▸Vague "sticking" sensation; may go unnoticed
  • ▸Nutrition and weight unaffected
  • ▸Near-normal manometry; mildly reduced DCI
  • ▸Mx: Dietary advice; slower eating; fluids with meals
●● Moderate
  • ▸Consistent dysphagia; solids and occasionally liquids
  • ▸Non-cardiac chest pain; regurgitation
  • ▸Nutritional impact; quality of life affected
  • ▸Manometry: ineffective oesophageal motility (IOM)
  • ▸Mx: Dietitian + SLT referral; consider PPIs
●●● Severe
  • ▸Dysphagia to solids and liquids; eating feared
  • ▸Significant weight loss; high aspiration risk
  • ▸Recurrent chest infections; debilitating spasm
  • ▸Manometry: absent contractility or pan-oesophageal pressurisation
  • ▸Mx: MDT; NG/PEG feeding; consider dilatation/POEM

Note: Corkscrew oesophagus and diffuse oesophageal spasm (DOS) are typically features of moderate-to-severe dysmotility. DCI = distal contractile integral; IOM = ineffective oesophageal motility; MDT = multidisciplinary team.

✚ Management Principles

  • Dietary modification — soft or pureed diet; small frequent meals; adequate hydration with meals
  • Postural advice — remain upright 30–60 minutes post-meal; elevate head of bed if reflux component present
  • Acid suppression — proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) where reflux-mediated symptoms contribute
  • Smooth muscle relaxants — calcium channel blockers or nitrates may reduce spasm in DOS; limited evidence in elderly cohorts
  • Speech and language therapy (SLT) — particularly where aspiration risk is elevated
  • Endoscopic or surgical intervention — reserved for achalasia or refractory spasm (e.g. pneumatic dilatation, POEM)
