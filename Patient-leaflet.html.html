Swallowing Difficulties
and the Ageing Gullet

Understanding why swallowing can become harder as we get older,
and what can be done to help.

🫁
As we age, the muscles and nerves in the food pipe (gullet or oesophagus) can gradually become less coordinated. This is a normal part of ageing and is called oesophageal dysmotility — which simply means the gullet is not moving food along as smoothly as it once did. It is more common after the age of 60 and is not the same as having a blockage or growth.
What does the gullet normally do?

When you swallow, your gullet squeezes in a wave-like motion to push food and drink down into your stomach. With age, these squeezes can become weaker, less coordinated, or happen out of sequence — meaning food moves more slowly or unevenly.

What might you notice?
  • Food feeling like it sticks in your chest or throat, especially bread, meat or dry foods
  • Difficulty swallowing — this may come and go at first
  • Chest discomfort or pain after eating — this is from the gullet, not the heart
  • Bringing food back up after swallowing
  • A feeling of a lump in your throat, even when not eating
  • Eating more slowly or avoiding certain foods
  • Unexpected weight loss if eating has become difficult
How serious can it be?

Dysmotility can range from very mild — barely noticeable — to more significant. Here is a simple guide:

🟢 Mild
  • Occasional sticking sensation
  • Solids only, comes and goes
  • Weight stays stable
  • Simple changes to eating habits usually help
🟡 Moderate
  • More regular swallowing difficulty
  • Some chest discomfort or regurgitation
  • May start to affect what you eat
  • Tests and specialist advice likely needed
🔴 Severe
  • Difficulty with most foods and liquids
  • Weight loss; eating becomes distressing
  • Risk of food or liquid entering the airway
  • Specialist team involvement essential
How is it investigated?

Your doctor may arrange one or more of the following:

  • Endoscopy (camera test) — a thin, flexible camera is passed into your gullet to look for any abnormalities. Sometimes a corkscrew or rippled appearance of the gullet wall is visible, caused by the muscles contracting irregularly.
  • Barium swallow — you swallow a chalky liquid while X-rays are taken, showing how it moves through your gullet in real time
  • Manometry — a thin tube measures the pressure and coordination of the gullet muscles as you swallow
  • pH / impedance test — checks whether stomach acid is coming back up (reflux) as a contributing factor
What to tell your doctor

It helps to tell your doctor: when the swallowing difficulty started, whether it is getting worse, and whether it affects solids, liquids or both.

Also mention any unintended weight loss, episodes of coughing or choking while eating or drinking, or any recent chest infections — these are important symptoms that need prompt assessment.

Keep a simple food diary for a week before your appointment if you can — noting what was difficult to swallow and when — as this is very helpful to your clinical team.

Tips to make eating easier
🍽️
Eat slowly Take smaller bites and chew thoroughly. Put your cutlery down between mouthfuls.
💧
Drink with meals Small sips of water help wash food through the gullet more easily.
🪑
Sit upright Stay seated and upright for at least 30–60 minutes after eating. Avoid lying down straight after meals.
🥣
Softer foods If certain foods cause problems, try softer alternatives — scrambled eggs, yoghurt, soups, mashed vegetables.
🛏️
Raise your bed head Propping up the head of your bed by a few inches can help if symptoms are worse at night.
🚫
Avoid triggers Spicy food, caffeine and alcohol can worsen symptoms for some people. Try reducing these and see if it helps.
When to seek urgent help

Please contact your GP or seek urgent care if you experience: sudden or rapidly worsening difficulty swallowing, choking on food or liquids, vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds, unexplained weight loss, or chest pain that is new or severe. These symptoms always need prompt medical review.
